\section{The Zipfian Distribution}
This distributions has had ample impact in the areas of quantitative linguistics and information theory \cite{powersApplicationsExplanationsZipfs1998a}, as well as within computer science in the fields of storage systems and \ac{RDBMS}\cite{grayQuicklyGeneratingBillionrecord1994}. It states that "(...) when a list when 
a list of measured values is sorted in decreasing order, the value of the $n$-th entry is often approximately inversely proportional to $n$" \cite{ZipfsLaw2025}. Mathematically, this is expressed by

\begin{equation}\label{eqt:zipfs_law}
    \textbf{word frequency} \propto \frac{1}{\textbf{word rank}}
\end{equation}

The form of interest for this research has an additional parameter $\alpha$, yielding

\begin{equation}\label{eqt:zm_law}
    \textbf{frequency} \propto \frac{1}{\textbf{rank}^\alpha}
\end{equation}

Before continuing onto defining the probability more rigorously, some terms are defined more precisely:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $N$ - the support size of the distribution
    \item $k$ - the rank of the variate
    \item $f_{theo}(k, N, \alpha)$ - the theoretical frequency of the $k$-th variate
\end{itemize}

With this, it' possible to write the following equation for the $PMF$ of the Zipfian distribution:
\begin{equation}\label{eqt:zm_prob}
    f_{theo}(k, N, \alpha) := 
 \begin{cases} 
      \frac{1}{H_{N,\alpha}} \frac{1}{k^\alpha} & 1 \leq k \leq N \\
      0 & k < 1 \, \text{or} \, N < k 
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $H_{N,\alpha}$ is defined by the generalized harmonic number

\begin{equation}\label{eqt:generalized_hn}
    H_{N, \alpha} = \sum_{k = 1}^{N} \frac{1}{k^\alpha}
\end{equation}

To give more concrete form to this distribution, we can visualize how the curve looks like for $N = 100$ and with an
exponent $\alpha = 1$ in Figure \ref{fig:zipf_pmf}.

\begin{figure}[t]

    \caption{Probability Mass Function of a Zipfian distribution with $N = 100$ and $\alpha = 1$}
    \label{fig:zipf_pmf}
\end{figure}

\section{Recognizing a Zipfian distribution}
One way to visually recognize whether a sample follows a Zipfian distribution or not is by using a plot in log-log scale.
This is justified by taking equation \ref{eqt:zm_prob} (assuming the probability is always well-defined) and applying
a log on both sides.

\begin{equation}
    \log{f(k,N,\alpha)} = -\alpha\log{k} - \log(H_{N,\alpha})
\end{equation}

Since this is clearly a linear form: $Y = mX + C$, we should expect data that was generated according to a Zipfian distribution
to have a linear form when transformed with the logarithm. Applying this on the last graph yields Figure \ref{fig:loglog_zipf}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \include{figures/zipfian_loglog}\caption{Application of log on data from \ref{fig:zipf_pmf}}\label{fig:loglog_zipf}
\end{figure}

It's a useful criterium for graphically evaluating if the Zipfian has the expected behaviour through the whole domain. It's not 
foolproof since other power laws also turn linear when transformed with the Zipfian, but since all generators analyse \textit{try} to generate 
such a distribution, the problem disappears.

\section{Discrete Variate Generation}

Theoretically defining a distribution is one aspect of the work, but the main goal is to generate
variates which follow a desired distribution. This can be represented by \ref{eq:rvg_definition}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:rvg_definition}
F(x) = Pr(X \leq x) \quad -\infty < x < \infty
\end{equation}

The theory for developing variate generators rely on the following assumptions \cite{kuhlHistoryRandomVariate2017}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item There exists a perfect uniform $(0,1)$ generator that produces a sequence of \textit{independent} random variables uniformly distributed in $(0,1)$
    \item Computers can save and manipulate real numbers
\end{itemize}

Which are directly violated once implementation in digital computers is considered. While not \textit{a priori} a problem, since 
computers always introduce different types of errors in numerical computations, it's important to consider by \textit{how much} these
assumptions are violated. Even more so if certain distributions exarcebate problems by this violation \cite{radicchiUnderestimatingExtremeEvents2014}.

There are many possible implementations and theoretical frameworks for creating discrete samplers, be them exact or approximative. Each method has its
advantages and disadvantages and in the next sections, they will be evaluated. It's important to note, however, that other approaches are possible, but have been omitted since
the ones present in the table \ref{tab:zipf_sampling} have already compared more favourably to them (see \cite{devroyeDiscreteUnivariateDistributions1986,kronmalAliasMethodGenerating1979,kuhlHistoryRandomVariate2017}).
A brief discussion of the methods is presented in the next section.

\subsection{Rejection Sampling}\label{subsec:rejection_sampling}
Rejection Sampling is an exact simulation method to generate observations from a distribution. 
It's working principle is quite simple: imagine plotting the probability density function of the distribution
to a large rectangular board. Onto this board, darts are thrown at random (assuming every position is equally as likely)
and the darts that land under the area of the curve are kept. After throwing enough darts, a clear pattern starts to form
that resembles the distribution \cite{RejectionSampling2024}. The graphs in Figure \ref{fig:rejection_sampling} illustrate this principle well - blue
points are accepted whereas red points are rejected using the previously described criterium.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/rejection_sampling.png}
    \caption{To generate these graphs, a random uniform sample is taken for the x-axis as well as another normalized one for the y-axis.}\label{fig:rejection_sampling}
\end{figure}

It's important to notice, however, that for this method to work, a \textit{bounding function} is needed. In the case of the Zipf, this could be simply $1/k^{-\alpha}$, which is easier to calculate than
the true Zipfian distribution. While the bounding function must be comparable to the original function, it must only \textit{be greater up to a constant factor} - this eliminates the need for the
generalized harmonic number, which is computationally expensive to calculate. A disadvantage of this method is that two random samples are required: one for the x-axis
, another one for the y-axis.

\subsection{Rejection-Inversion Sampling}\label{subsec:rejection_inversion}
Rejection-Inversion Sampling due to Hörmann and Derflinger \cite{hormannRejectioninversionGenerateVariates1996}  is a more efficient version of the Rejection Sampling method. It's based on the following observations regarding 
the naive rejection method:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A naive rejection algorithm requires two samples to generate the target variate.
    \item The Zipfian is bounded by a monotonically decreasing and convex function, which makes it possible to invert the bounding function.
\end{itemize}

These two observations lead to the following principle:

\vspace{0.3cm}

\fbox{
    \parbox{0.83\linewidth}{
        Generate a random variate $u$ from the uniform distribution $U(0,1)$ and invert the bounding function to find the corresponding $x = H^{-1}(u)$.
        Define a bounding area around the nearest integer $k$ (with radius $a$).
        If $x \geq x_k$ and $x \leq k + a $, accept $X$ as a sample. Otherwise, reject it.\footnotemark
    }
}
\footnotetext{This is a simplified version of the algorithm. For a more detailed explanation, see \cite{hormannRejectioninversionGenerateVariates1996}}
\vspace{0.3cm}

To visualize the principle used, consider figure \ref{fig:rejection_inversion}: after $k$ was found using the inversion method, the bounding area is defined for radius $a$. 
This creates an interval of $k - a$ and $k + a$, which is then used to accept or reject the sample $x$. Typically, $a$ is chosen to be $1/2$ \cite{hormannRejectioninversionGenerateVariates1996}.

\begin{figure}[t]

    \caption{Rejection-Inversion Sampling for a Zipfian distribution}\label{fig:rejection_inversion}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Marsaglia's and Tsang's Condensed Table Lookup}\label{subsec:condensed_table}
To generate a variate from any discrete distribution, it's possible to populate with as many occurences of a given key as it occurs in the frequency table and then use a uniform sample to find the corresponding variate. This is called a table method \cite{marsagliaFastGenerationDiscrete2004}.
As an example, consider figure \ref{fig:lookup_table}: One takes a sample from an uniform \ac{PRNG} and uses it to access an index in the array. 

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=9cm]{figures/marsaglia.png}
    \caption{The table contains the frequency of each index, whereas the array is populated 
    with as many entries as there occurrencies of each index. The sample "2" was generated, which is used to access the 2nd position in the array}
    \label{fig:lookup_table}
\end{figure}

This approach has a big limitation: the size of the array is $O(n)$, which is not feasible for large support $n$. 
To circumvent this, Marsaglia and Tsang proposed a \textit{condensed table lookup method}\cite{marsagliaFastGenerationDiscrete2004}, which is based on the following principle:

\vspace{0.2cm}
\fbox{
    \parbox{0.83\linewidth}{
        A probability is represented by a floating-point number $p$ with $d$ decimal places. For example, assuming $d = 2$, 
        the entry $1$ from the last figure has probability $p(1) = 0.28$. To realize this probability, a table of size $10^2$ is required. 
        The trick to reduce space usage is to break this table down onto $d$ tables instead of using one big table with $100$ entries. 
        To do this, each probability gets split into $d$ digits $m$, which respectively get $m$ entries in the corresponding table.  Now, generating a uniform integer from the interval $[1,100]$, it's possible to access the correct table and generate the variate.
        \footnotemark
    }
}
\vspace{0.2cm}
\footnotetext{This is a simplified version of the algorithm. For a more detailed explanation, see \cite{marsagliaFastGenerationDiscrete2004}}

\subsection{Jim Gray's Approximative Method}\label{subsec:jim_gray}
Jim Gray's method \cite{grayQuicklyGeneratingBillionrecord1994} is a fast approximative way to generate samples from a Zipfian
 distribution. It's based on an approximation of the generalized harmonic number using integration for the first
most frequent elements and a exponential approximation for the tail. A uniform sample is then mapped onto an index using inversion.
Figure \ref{fig:jim_gray} makes this idea clear by having two different areas - the first uses the precise discrete values, whereas the second uses an exponential approximation.

\section{Error Metrics}
To begin comparing the different methods introduced previosly, it's necessary to first define the framework used to evaluate them. Drawing from the literature 
\cite{clausetPowerlawDistributionsEmpirical2009,leydoldErrorDetectionNonUniforma,monahanAccuracyRandomNumber1985}, the following principles are used:

\begin{mdframed}
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Calculate the theoretical distribution for the given parameters: $N$ - support size, and $\alpha$ - skew
            \item Generate a empirical distribution with the developed tool as explained in chapter \ref{chapter:approach} 
            \item Use a \textbf{goodness-of-fit test} to compare the generated distribution to the theoretical one
        \end{enumerate}
        \label{box:accuracy}
\end{mdframed}

This framework assumes a "black-box" view of the generating processes and treat the generated samples as a dataset that should be fit to the distribution we presume it was sampled from - 
in our case, the Zipfian. At the heart of this comparison is a \textbf{goodness-of-fit test}, which tells whether the empirical distribution matches the expected distribution closely enough.
This requires a comparison metric between the two distributions, which can be thought of as a function that takes two distributions and returns a number that quantifies the difference between them. 
This idea can be made more concrete by looking at the example given in \ref{fig:accuracy-measure}. If the blue curve represents the theoretical distribution and the orange curve the empirical one,
the shaded area represents the difference between the two. The smaller this area, the better the empirical distribution fits the theoretical one.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \input{figures/accuracy_measure.tex}
    \caption{Accuracy Measure}\label{fig:accuracy-measure}
\end{figure}

For the purpose of this work, two measures are going to be used: the \textbf{Kolmogorov-Smirnov} distance and the \textbf{Total Variation} distance \cite{marsagliaEvaluatingKolmogorovsDistribution2003,leydoldErrorDetectionNonUniforma}.
The former is used in the cited literature previously and has the advantage of being a non-parametric test, which means it doesn't require any assumptions about the underlying distribution.
The latter is a more general measure of the difference between two probability distributions and is more resistant against outliers.

\paragraph{Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test}
While there is much theory behind the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the good-of-fit measure is simply the maximum difference - $D_{n}(P,Q)$ - between the empirical and theoretical
distributions. A formal definition can be found in Equation \ref{eq:ks-statistic}. It can be understood best with the graph in Figure \ref{fig:ks-test}. This distance 
measure is compared with a theoretical distribution evaluated for the same parameters (see Equation \ref{eq:ks-cdf}).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \input{figures/ks-test.tex}
    \caption{Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test}\label{fig:ks-test}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
D_n = \sup_x |P(x) - Q(x)|
\label{eq:ks-statistic}
\end{equation}

As explained before, a theoretical distribution is associated with this measure so as to check whether the two distributions truly match.

\begin{equation}
    F_n = P[D_n \leq x | \mathcal{H}_0] \quad \text{for} \, x \in [0,1]
    \label{eq:ks-cdf}
\end{equation}

As this function is quite hard to accurately calculate and approximations are used for big sample sizes. The treatment given is due to Marsaglia (see \cite{marsagliaEvaluatingKolmogorovsDistribution2003})
and is the choice of implementation for the \textit{SciPy} library \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} used to generate the metric.

paragraph{Total Variation Distance}
The total variation distance is defined as the sum of all absolute differences in the probability mass function of two distributions. More precisely, 
given two (discrete) distributions $P$ and $Q$ defined in $X$

\begin{equation}
    d_{TV}(P,Q) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{x \in X} |P(x) - Q(x)|    
    \label{eq:total-variation}
\end{equation}

It ranges from $0$ to $1$, where $0$ means the distributions are equal and $1$ means they are completely different. Interpreting it as a percentage allows statements as:
"About $y\%$ of the probability mass differs between the theoretical and empirical distribution". To better understand how this measure looks visually, 
observe that this measure would be equivalent to the shaded area in figure \ref{fig:accuracy-measure}.

An important property of the total variation distance is that with growing sample sizes,
the distance between the empirical and theoretical distribution should converge to 0 for 
all $x$ \cite{kengEmpiricalDistributionFunction2016}. Associated to this metric is the \textit{Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality}
\cite{devroyeInequalitiesAlternateDistance1996}, which gives a bound on the probability that the distance between the empirical and theoretical distribution is greater than a certain value. 

\paragraph{Why the tests are not perfect}
While these tests are widely used in the literature, they are not perfect. This work stops short of making hypothesis test due to the bounding conditions
of the rejection criteria used. Both metric tests converge fast to 0 with growing sample sizes, which makes rejecting the null hypothesis very hard. 
Therefore, the analysis will limit itself to presenting which methods are \textit{more correct} than others and how \textit{wrong} they are in absolute terms. 
For the purpose of this work this approach suffices, as it can be determined how innacurate latency measurements will be compared to the real distribution and estimates can be made
on how much this will affect the system, e.g. \textit{if the probability of tail indexes is off by $10\%$, latency measures will be off by $5\%$}. Additionally, 
other techniques will be used to ensure the correctness of the Zipfian implementations.
